Fault Classes in Fiber Optic Cable Traces
Fiber optic networks can experience various types of faults or events that impact signal performance. By
analyzing an Optical Time Domain Reflectometer (OTDR) trace or similar diagnostics, technicians classify
these events into specific fault classes. Each class has distinct characteristics in terms of signal loss and
reflectance (light reflected back). Below, we describe each fault class (numbered 0 through 7) and their
typical signs on a fiber trace:
Class 0: Normal / No Fault
Description: This represents a healthy fiber link with no faults. The OTDR trace appears as a normal
baseline with gradual attenuation over distance due to fiber loss, but no abnormal drops or reflections
are present. The loss is approximately 0 (beyond the expected steady attenuation), and there are no sudden
events indicated . Essentially, the fiber shows a continuous signal with an expected end-of-trace at
the fiber’s termination. Any minor fluctuations are within normal range, and no significant reflective peaks
appear.
Typical Signs: A stable baseline trace with minimal loss and no spurious reflections. The position of any
known connector or the fiber end is as expected, and there is no excess attenuation at any point. In
summary, the fiber is operating normally, with a clean trace and nearly zero unexpected loss.
Class 1: Fiber Tapping
Description: Fiber tapping refers to an unauthorized interception of the optical signal, often for
eavesdropping. It is typically implemented by bending the fiber or inserting a passive optical coupler to
siphon off some light without breaking the fiber. On an OTDR trace, a tapping event manifests as a
localized drop in signal power (insertion loss) with little or no reflective spike . This is because
tapping devices (like bend couplers or splice-in splitters) are designed to extract light stealthily and
minimize reflections that would otherwise alert monitoring systems . The loss is usually moderate (not
as high as a cut, but noticeable above normal attenuation).
Typical Signs: A localized disturbance on the trace where the backscatter signal level drops suddenly by a
modest amount. Importantly, reflectance is low or absent – unlike a break or connector, a tap doesn’t
produce a strong reflection back to the OTDR. The loss event might resemble a splice or bend in magnitude,
but the lack of a corresponding reflective peak is a clue. In practice, fiber tapping causes a moderate
insertion loss (due to the coupler extracting some light) but leaves very little Fresnel reflection, making it
harder to detect via OTDR alone . Network security systems may use this signature (unexplained loss
with no reflection) to flag potential fiber tapping incidents.

Class 2: Bad Splice
Description: A splice is a junction where two fiber ends are joined, either by fusion splicing (melting fibers
together) or mechanical splicing. A good splice has very low loss and minimal reflectance (especially if
fusion spliced properly). A bad splice, however, occurs when the splice is done poorly – for example,
misaligned cores, air gaps, dirt in the splice, or poor fiber end-face quality. This results in excess loss at the
splice point and possibly a small reflection. On the trace, it appears as a localized event with higherthan-normal loss at that splice location, and there may be a noticeable but small reflective spike if the
splice surfaces are not perfectly mated . Mechanical splices or badly polished fiber ends are more likely
to show reflectance than a perfect fusion splice .
Typical Signs: A sudden drop (step down) in power at a specific point along the fiber, greater than what a
normal splice would cause. The insertion loss is significant – indicating a mismatch or gap – and a minor
reflection may be present . The reflectance of a bad splice is usually lower than that of a connector, but
it’s higher than a good fusion splice (which is nearly non-reflective). Technicians seeing an OTDR trace would
identify a bad splice if an event shows, say, a few dB of loss and perhaps a reflectance on the order of -50 dB
to -40 dB (a small peak). Essentially, excess loss with slight reflection at a splice point is the hallmark of
Class 2. This often implies the splice should be reworked or cleaned to reduce loss .
Class 3: Bending Event
Description: Bending events are caused by macrobends or tight bends in the fiber that induce loss. When
a fiber is bent beyond its minimum bend radius, some light leaks out of the fiber core, leading to
attenuation. Importantly, this bending loss is a radiative loss, not a discrete break, so it typically does not
create a reflective event on the OTDR trace . Instead of a sharp spike, a bend can cause a gradual or
sudden drop in backscatter level (depending on if the bend is distributed or at a point). It often appears as a
broad loss region or a step-down without reflection.
Typical Signs: A gradual or moderate drop in signal level on the trace over a segment, or a step-loss at a
specific location, with no corresponding reflectance peak . The loss from a bend might be a few dB
and can be spread out if the fiber is looped or coiled (making the trace slope change), or it can look like a
splice-like drop if it’s a single tight bend. Unlike breaks or connectors, the OTDR does not record a strong
reflection because the fiber remains continuous and no significant Fresnel reflection occurs at the bend .
In summary, Class 3 is identified by medium attenuation without reflection, usually correlating with a
physical fiber bend or kink. Removing or re-routing the bend will restore the signal power if this is indeed
the fault.
Class 4: Dirty Connector
Description: A dirty connector refers to the end-face of a fiber connector being contaminated (with dust,
oil, debris) or otherwise not clean. Connectors join fiber segments and normally should have low loss and a
controlled reflectance. But contamination or damage on the connector’s surface causes improper mating,
leading to higher insertion loss and erratic reflections. Essentially, a dirty (or damaged) connector becomes
a high-loss connector with unpredictable reflective behavior . It’s still a connector interface, so it will
reflect some light (especially if it’s a Physical Contact, PC, polish connector), but because of the dirt, the
reflection may be higher than normal and the connection’s loss is also elevated beyond spec. The

Typical Signs: On an OTDR trace, a dirty connector often shows up similarly to a normal connector event
but with excess attenuation and higher reflectance than expected . Typically, you’d see a reflective
spike (indicating a connector interface), but the drop right before the spike (the loss through the connector)

dB, whereas a dirty connector could show several dB of loss and reflectance maybe in the high -30s or
worse (a larger reflection, meaning lower return loss) . The term “messy reflectance” means the
reflective signal might not be a clean single peak; there could be multiple small reflections or an unstable
return power due to the contamination. In practice, high reflection and insertion loss at a connector
indicates it may be dirty or damaged. Cleaning the connector end-faces typically resolves this fault,
restoring low loss and stable reflectance.
Class 5: Fiber Cut
Description: A fiber cut is a severe fault where the fiber is physically broken or severed. This results in an
abrupt end to the fiber link at the break point. On an OTDR trace, a fiber cut is characterized by a sudden,
large loss event followed by the loss of signal (the trace flatlines after that point, since no further fiber is
detected). In many cases, a fiber break also acts like an “open end” of fiber, which causes a significant
Fresnel reflection because the light hits the air interface at the break and a portion is reflected straight back.
Thus, a fiber cut often appears as a very large reflective spike followed by the end of the trace . If the
cut is near the end of the fiber (e.g., someone cut the cable near its terminus), it may simply look like the
fiber ended early with a strong reflection at that new end. If the cut is in the middle, everything beyond that
point will not be visible in the trace.
Typical Signs: An abrupt, high loss event – essentially a drop to near-zero power – at a certain distance,
indicating the fiber stops there. Often this is accompanied by a strong reflectance (a large peak on the
OTDR) at the break location . The reflectance from an open fiber break can be on the order of -20 dB or
even -14 dB (which corresponds to a few percent of the light being reflected) . After this point, no further
fiber backscatter is seen, because the rest of the link is unreachable. In summary, the OTDR trace for a
Class 5 fault will show a sudden end-of-trace at the break distance with a huge loss and typically a high
reflection. This is unequivocal evidence of a broken fiber. The distance reading gives the break location so
technicians can go repair it (often by splicing the fiber back or replacing the broken segment).
Class 6: PC Connector (Good Connector)
Description: “PC connector” stands for Physical Contact connector, which is a common type of fiber
connector (usually with a flat or slightly curved polished end-face that mates fibers directly). A good PC
connector is not a fault per se, but it is often marked on OTDR traces as an event because it does cause a
small reflection and a small insertion loss. This class is included to identify normal connector events in the
link. A Class 6 event indicates a proper, clean connector pair in the fiber path, typically at a known location
(such as a patch panel or an end termination) . The connector causes a reflective event due to the
refractive index discontinuity (glass to air to glass interface), but because it’s a proper contact, the
reflectance is moderate and the loss is low.

Typical Signs: On the trace, a PC connector shows up as a sharp reflective spike (Fresnel reflection) paired
with a small drop in power right before it (insertion loss) . The reflectance of a good PC connector
might be around -40 dB (return loss ~40 dB), producing a noticeable but not extreme peak . The loss
might be around 0.2–0.5 dB typically. This event will be at the expected position – for example, if you know
there is a connector at 2.0 km in the link, the OTDR will show an event there matching those characteristics.
In essence, Class 6 is a clean connector event with low loss and a reflective signature. It’s usually not a
problem to fix but is recorded for completeness or for distinguishing from actual faults.
Class 7: Reflector
Description: Class 7 refers to a highly reflective event in the fiber link. This is an event where an unusually
large amount of light is reflected back. It could be caused by an open connector or fiber end, a broken
fiber facing air, or a purposefully installed reflective device (like a fiber Bragg grating or a fiber end
mirror used for monitoring). Unlike standard connectors or splices, a reflector stands out due to its very
high reflectance value. On an OTDR, reflectance is often measured in negative dB: the closer to 0 dB, the
stronger the reflection. A strong reflector might have reflectance on the order of -20 dB or higher (which is
considered high reflectance, meaning a large percentage of light is reflected) . This is much more
reflective than a typical connector (which might be -40 dB or lower).
Typical Signs: A reflector appears as a prominent, tall spike on the OTDR trace, higher than any other
reflective events. It may even saturate the OTDR receiver if extremely strong. The loss associated might be
minimal or irrelevant – for instance, an open end will reflect strongly but you can’t measure its insertion loss
because it’s the end of fiber. If the reflector is mid-span (some device or fault), there could be a drop and
then a huge reflection. In summary, Class 7 events are identified by an unusually high reflected power. An
example is an open connector left unplugged: it can reflect about 4% of the light, corresponding to roughly
-14 dB reflectance . Such a spike might also lead to ghost artifacts on the trace (echoes) due to the
strong reflection, but those ghosts are not actual separate events . The presence of a strong reflector is
usually undesirable (it can indicate an open port or break) unless intentionally used for monitoring.
Technicians would investigate any Class 7 reflective event to ensure it’s either a known end-of-line or
remove the cause of reflection if it’s unintended.
Sources: The characteristics above are based on standard fiber optic testing knowledge and OTDR trace
interpretation guidelines from industry sources. For example, connectors and open fiber ends create
reflective spikes on an OTDR , whereas bends and good fusion splices only attenuate the signal
without reflection . Contaminated connectors are a common cause of high reflection and loss in
networks . Fiber tapping events mimic bends by causing loss with little reflection . Understanding
these signatures allows technicians and automated systems (including AI models in Retrieval-Augmented
Generation setups) to identify the fault type and take appropriate action. By feeding this comprehensive
description of fault classes into an AI’s knowledge base, the system can better diagnose fiber link issues
from observed symptoms.
